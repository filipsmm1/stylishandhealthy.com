Azelaic Acid for Dark Spots and Acne: Does It Actually Work?

what the evidence says about PIH, PIE, acne, strength, irritation and realistic timelines

By Filip · Founder and editorial researcher, Stylish & Healthy

Published: July 28, 2026 | Last updated: July 28, 2026

This article is not medically reviewed. It was checked against current acne guidance, FDA prescribing information and peer-reviewed clinical studies. It is educational, not personal medical advice.

Stylish & Healthy17 min readEvidence-based ingredient guide
Quick answer

Yes, azelaic acid can help acne and post-acne dark spots. Its strongest use case is mild-to-moderate acne with flat brown or gray post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. It may also improve some red or pink post-acne marks, but the evidence is newer and less settled. The most convincing acne and discoloration studies use 15% or 20% formulas. A 10% cosmetic product may still be useful, especially for beginners, but it does not have the same depth of clinical evidence. Expect weeks of consistent use, not an overnight fade.[1, 2, 4, 5, 6]

The realistic version

Azelaic acid is not a scar eraser. It can improve active breakouts and flat discoloration. It cannot lift a pitted acne scar or flatten a raised scar. Before choosing a treatment, use our PIH vs PIE vs acne scars guide to check whether the main issue is color or texture.

Does azelaic acid for dark spots actually work?

Azelaic acid is one of those ingredients that sounds almost too convenient. It is used for acne. It is used for rosacea. It can calm inflammation, influence pigment production and help keep pores from clogging. That does not make every claim about it true, but it does make it unusually useful when breakouts and the marks left behind are happening at the same time.

The American Academy of Dermatology includes topical azelaic acid among the recommended therapies for acne. The current guideline describes it as an option that can address acne while helping fade the dark spots that remain after a blemish clears.[1]

Human studies support that basic idea. A 2024 randomized, double-blind trial enrolled 72 people with mild-to-moderate acne and acne-related post-inflammatory erythema or hyperpigmentation. Participants used 15% azelaic acid gel or a matching vehicle twice daily for 12 weeks. The azelaic acid group improved across several measures of acne, red marks and brown marks, with clearer pigment and redness changes appearing later in the study rather than immediately.[4]

An earlier 16-week study of 15% gel also reported improvement in both acne and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. Another randomized study found 20% azelaic acid cream comparable to 5% topical tranexamic acid for acne-related hyperpigmentation. Tranexamic acid had a better safety profile during the first month, while side-effect frequency was not significantly different at weeks 8 and 12.[5, 6]

Azelaic acid works best when you treat it as a steady acne-and-marks treatment, not a seven-day brightening challenge.

How azelaic acid affects pigmentation

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, usually shortened to PIH, forms when inflammation pushes pigment-producing cells into overdrive. The result is a flat brown, gray-brown or darker mark where acne used to be. Azelaic acid can interfere with tyrosinase, an enzyme involved in melanin production, and appears to be more selective toward unusually active pigment cells than normal surrounding skin.[11]

That is why azelaic acid is not best described as bleach. The goal is to reduce the excess pigment response that follows inflammation, not strip all pigment from the skin. In real life, fading still depends on how deep the pigment sits, how often new acne appears, how irritating the rest of the routine is and how consistently the skin is protected from sunlight.

Red or pink marks are different. They are often described as post-inflammatory erythema, or PIE, and are more closely tied to residual inflammation and visible blood vessels than excess melanin. Azelaic acid may still help because it has anti-inflammatory activity. The 2024 trial found improvement in measures related to both PIE and PIH, but one study is not enough to promise the same result for every red mark.[4]

Why it is also useful for acne itself

Fading an old mark matters, but preventing the next one matters more. Every new inflamed pimple can restart the cycle, especially if your skin tends to hold pigment for months.

Azelaic acid does more than brighten. Laboratory and prescribing information describe antimicrobial activity, anti-inflammatory effects and normalization of keratinization, the process that can contribute to blocked pores and microcomedones. In plain English, it can target several parts of acne formation at once.[2, 12]

That dual action is the reason it makes sense for someone who keeps thinking, “My acne is finally improving, but every spot leaves a mark.” A single tolerable active that addresses breakouts and discoloration can be easier to use consistently than a crowded routine with four aggressive products.

If the same painful blemish keeps returning in one exact area, the explanation may be more complicated than a clogged pore. Read Why Acne Keeps Returning in the Same Spot.

10% vs 15% vs 20% azelaic acid

The percentage matters, but it is not the only thing that matters. A well-formulated 10% emulsion that you use every day can outperform a gritty 20% cream that makes you quit after a week. Vehicle, pH, supporting ingredients and your skin barrier all affect how a product behaves.

Cosmetic does not mean prescription-equivalent

The FDA classifies products by intended use and claims. A cosmetic azelaic acid product and a prescription drug can contain the same named ingredient without having the same formulation, evidence, labeling or regulatory review.[10]

There is no universal rule that everyone should start at the highest percentage. If your skin is reactive, dry, over-exfoliated or already using a retinoid, a lower strength or less frequent schedule may be the smarter starting point.

How often to use azelaic acid

Prescription labels commonly instruct twice-daily use. Azelex 20% cream is applied in a thin layer morning and evening, and its label notes that many patients with inflammatory acne see improvement within about four weeks. Finacea 15% gel is also labeled for twice-daily application for its approved use.[2, 3]

That does not mean sensitive skin needs to jump straight into twice a day. The Azelex label specifically says persistent irritation may require once-daily use or a temporary pause. A practical approach is to start every other evening or once daily, then increase only if the skin remains comfortable. This slower ramp-up is sensible barrier management, not proof that one exact schedule is best for everyone.[2]

  1. Cleanse gently. Use a mild cleanser and pat the skin dry. Applying to damp or freshly irritated skin may increase stinging.
  2. Use a thin layer. More product does not produce faster fading. Avoid the eyes, mouth and broken skin.
  3. Add moisturizer. Apply moisturizer after azelaic acid, or use a moisturizer-first sandwich if direct application feels too sharp.
  4. Use sunscreen every morning. Without reliable photoprotection, dark spots can keep deepening while you are trying to fade them.

What azelaic acid before and after usually looks like

Early acne improvement can happen around the first month. Dark spots usually need longer. In the 2024 trial, clearer pigment and redness changes appeared around weeks 8 and 12. The 16-week study continued to show improvement through the full treatment period.[2, 4, 5]

Take progress photos in the same lighting every four weeks. Daily mirror checking is unreliable because lighting, redness and recent irritation can make the skin look different from one hour to the next.

Can you use azelaic acid with salicylic acid?

Yes, but compatibility is not the same as tolerance. A randomized trial has evaluated a professional peel combining 20% salicylic acid and 20% azelaic acid. The combination improved acne, especially inflammatory lesions early in treatment. That tells us the ingredients are not chemically incompatible. It does not mean everyone should layer strong leave-on versions at home every night.[8]

A gentler option is to use a salicylic acid cleanser and leave-on azelaic acid, or alternate a 2% salicylic acid product with azelaic acid on different nights. Learn what salicylic acid actually does in The Truth About Salicylic Acid 2%.

Can you use azelaic acid with glycolic acid?

Again, yes, but carefully. A randomized 24-week study compared 20% azelaic acid plus 15% or 20% glycolic acid with 4% hydroquinone for facial hyperpigmentation in darker-skinned patients. The combination performed comparably, showing that the two ingredients can be used together in a structured regimen.[7]

That study used high strengths under clinical conditions. At home, a 7% glycolic toner plus azelaic acid can still create burning, peeling and barrier disruption if you start both at once. Alternate nights, reduce frequency or keep glycolic acid to one or two nights per week. Our The Ordinary Glycolic Acid 7% review explains who should be cautious with frequent use.

Do not chase faster fading with more irritation

Inflamed, peeling skin can look temporarily brighter while becoming more vulnerable to redness and new PIH. If the routine burns, stings for a long time or leaves persistent tightness, simplify it.

Irritation and sensitive-skin considerations

Azelaic acid is often gentler than strong retinoids or frequent exfoliating acids, but gentler does not mean non-irritating. Official labels list burning, stinging, tingling, itching, dryness, scaling and redness among the possible reactions. These effects are often most noticeable during the first few weeks.[2, 3]

Short-lived mild tingling can happen. Persistent burning, swelling, blistering, severe redness or an itchy rash is not something to push through. Stop the product and seek medical advice if the reaction is significant.

Official labeling also notes isolated reports of hypopigmentation and advises people with dark complexions to monitor for unexpected lighter patches. That warning does not mean azelaic acid is generally unsafe for deeper skin tones. It means no ingredient should be marketed as risk-free for every person.[2, 3]

If your face is oily but also feels tight, flaky or stingy, adding more acids is usually the wrong move. Use our guide to oily but dehydrated skin to rebuild a calmer base routine first.

Why sunscreen changes whether azelaic acid seems to work

Dark-spot treatment starts with sunscreen. That is not filler advice. The American Academy of Dermatology states that effective treatment for dark spots begins with sunscreen because ultraviolet and visible light can worsen pigmentation and slow clearing.[9]

Use a broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher every morning. For stubborn hyperpigmentation, especially in medium and deep skin tones, a tinted formula containing iron oxides can add protection against visible light. Reapply when you are outdoors, sweating or exposed for long periods.[9]

Our Beauty of Joseon Relief Sun review looks at a popular untinted sunscreen and its filters. It can be a comfortable daily option for many people, but it is not an iron-oxide tinted sunscreen, so choose based on your pigmentation needs and what you will actually wear consistently.

Frequently asked questions

Does 10% azelaic acid work for dark spots?

It may help, especially with mild PIH and acne, but the strongest clinical evidence for acne-related outcomes is concentrated around 15% and 20%. A 10% product can still be a sensible starting point if it is comfortable enough to use consistently.

How long does azelaic acid take to fade acne marks?

Expect roughly 8 to 16 weeks for meaningful dark-spot changes. Active acne may begin improving earlier, sometimes around four weeks. Deeper pigment can take much longer.[2, 4, 5, 9]

Is azelaic acid better for PIH or PIE?

The most established rationale is for PIH because azelaic acid affects excess pigment production. A 2024 trial also found improvement in acne-related PIE, likely through anti-inflammatory effects, but the evidence for red marks is newer.[4, 11]

Can azelaic acid remove pitted acne scars?

No. It can improve discoloration over a scar and may make the skin look more even, but it cannot lift a deep indentation or remove raised scar tissue. Structural scars need scar-directed assessment.

Can I use azelaic acid every day?

Many products are designed for daily use, and prescription labels may direct twice-daily use. Start less often if your skin is sensitive, then increase only if the skin stays comfortable.[2, 3]

Should azelaic acid be used in the morning or at night?

Either can work. Morning use pairs well with sunscreen. Night use may be easier if you already have several morning layers. The best schedule is the one you can follow without irritation.

Is tingling normal?

Brief mild tingling can happen, especially early on. Ongoing burning, strong redness, swelling, blistering or a persistent itchy rash is a reason to stop and seek medical advice.[2, 3]

When should I see a dermatologist?

Get professional help for painful nodules, new scars, severe or widespread acne, unexpected lighter patches, a major reaction, or discoloration that is not improving after several months of a consistent gentle routine.
