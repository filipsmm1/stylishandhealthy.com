Does Volufiline Work? The Plumping Serum, Checked

The "filler-like" ingredient behind The Ordinary’s trending serum rests on cell studies and one small, unpublished trial on the bust.

By Filip · Founder and editorial researcher

Published and checked: November 21, 2026

This is an evidence review, not a personal product test. It separates manufacturer data from independent research.

Stylish & Healthy6 min readResearch-first guide
Quick answer

There is no good published evidence that Volufiline visibly plumps the face. The main clinical figure, an "up to 8.4%" volume increase, comes from a small study of about 28 women, run by the ingredient's manufacturer and never published in a peer-reviewed journal. The idea behind it, that a plant compound makes fat cells store more fat, comes from cell experiments. The Ordinary's serum is a pleasant, water-free emollient oil, but expecting it to work like filler will probably disappoint you.

What is Volufiline?

Volufiline is a trademarked cosmetic ingredient made by Sederma, the French supplier behind the Matrixyl peptides. It is an extract of the root of Anemarrhena asphodeloides, a plant used in traditional Chinese medicine, delivered in an oil.12 The active compound Sederma highlights is sarsasapogenin.

The pitch is unusual. Most "plumping" skincare works on the surface by drawing in water or forming a smoothing film. Volufiline was marketed to do something deeper: encourage fat cells under the skin to grow and store more fat, giving a "lipofilling-like" effect. Much of the marketing, and the main clinical test, focused on the bust and buttocks.2

Search interest jumped in 2026 after The Ordinary launched Volufiline 92% + Pal-Isoleucine 1%, marketed for hollow areas such as under the eyes, the temples and the cheeks.1

What's actually in The Ordinary's serum?

The full ingredient list is short:1

Hydrogenated polyisobutene, caprylyl glycol, polyglyceryl-3 stearate, palmitoyl isoleucine, Anemarrhena asphodeloides root extract, Rhus succedanea fruit wax, tocopherol.

  • "92% Volufiline" doesn't mean 92% plant extract. Volufiline is a ready-made blend sold to brands. The first ingredient, the one present in the largest amount, is hydrogenated polyisobutene, a smooth emollient oil. The Anemarrhena extract sits fifth.
  • Palmitoyl isoleucine (1%) is a fatty acid attached to an amino acid. The brand says it supports a plump look. We couldn't find independent human studies on it.
  • The rest are a preservative booster, an emulsifier, a plant wax and vitamin E.

In other words, what your skin feels straight away is mostly a silky, occlusive oil. That can make skin look smoother and more supple for a few hours, as any good emollient does. That effect is real, but it isn't fat growth.

The evidence, claim by claim

Claim 1: it makes fat cells store more fat

Sederma's mechanism data comes from experiments on fat-precursor cells in a dish. Published, independent research on Anemarrhena compounds and fat cells exists, but it doesn't line up neatly with the marketing. A 2009 study isolated compounds from the same root and found that one of them blocked fat-cell development in lab cells.3 A 2021 mouse study gave sarsasapogenin by mouth and found it reduced inflammation in fat tissue and improved insulin resistance.4 Neither tested a cream on human skin, and neither showed fat growth.

That doesn't disprove Sederma's results. It shows the published science on this plant is about metabolism and inflammation, not about growing facial fat from a serum.

Claim 2: "up to 8.4% more volume"

This figure comes from Sederma's own clinical test on female volunteers with a small bust, using Volufiline at 5% for 28 and 56 days. Dermatologists who looked at it pointed out how small it was (28 people) and how modest the change was.2 We could not find it published in a peer-reviewed journal, so outside researchers can't check how volume was measured, whether there was a proper control, or how many people saw no change at all. "Up to" also means 8.4% was the best result, not the typical one.

Claim 3: it plumps under-eye hollows and cheeks

We found no published, controlled study of Volufiline on the face. The facial claims are extrapolated from the body study and the cell work.

Why facial volume is a hard target

Facial fat isn't one even layer. It sits in separate pockets, some just under the skin and some deep under the muscles, and these pockets change at different rates as we age.5 Under-eye hollows and flatter cheeks are often driven by the deeper pockets and by bone changes, not only by the thin fat just under the surface.

Skin is also built to keep things out. Small molecules get through more easily, and a long-standing rule of thumb is that compounds above roughly 500 daltons barely penetrate at all.6 Even a compound small enough to enter the skin still has to reach the fat beneath it in a meaningful dose. Nobody has shown that happening with Volufiline on the face.

That's why dermatologists treat facial volume loss with fillers, fat grafting or biostimulatory injections, which put material exactly where the volume is missing. A serum can't aim like that.

If it did work as claimed

An ingredient that truly grew fat wherever you applied it would raise its own questions. Would it make you fuller in spots you didn't want, or lumpy if applied unevenly? The lack of reports of anything like that is another hint that the real-world effect is small.

If you still want to try it

  • Treat it as an emollient serum. It can make dry under-eyes and cheeks feel smoother and look more supple. Judge it on that.
  • Take photos in the same light before you start and after eight weeks, the length of the manufacturer's study. Mirror impressions are unreliable.
  • Apply it after water-based products and before your moisturizer, or at night as one of the last steps. Because it's oil-based, it may slide under sunscreen. If makeup or SPF starts to pill, use it at night only.
  • Patch test first, especially near the eyes.7
  • Don't stop sunscreen or retinoids for it. Daily sun protection and a retinoid have far better evidence for the skin changes that make the face look older.

If hollows bother you a lot, a consultation with a dermatologist or a qualified injector will tell you whether the cause is fat, bone, pigment or shadow. That answer is worth more than another serum.

Frequently asked questions

Does Volufiline work under the eyes?

There are no published facial studies. Any smoothing you see is most likely from the oil-based formula, not from new fat.

Is Volufiline a natural filler?

No. Fillers are injected into specific spots and have plenty of clinical data. Volufiline is a topical plant extract with one small, unpublished clinical test.

Is Volufiline safe?

Serious side effects haven't been reported, and the formula is fairly simple. As with any new product, patch test, and stop if you get redness, itching or bumps.

Can I use it with retinol or vitamin C?

There are no known interactions. Apply water-based serums first and the oil-based Volufiline serum after them.

Is it worth the money?

If you enjoy a silky emollient serum, it may be. If you're buying it to fill hollows, the evidence doesn't support that.
