Is Your Glass Skin Routine Doing Too Much?

Two studies presented at the 2026 European dermatology congress looked at viral glass skin advice and the patients it sent to clinics.

By Filip · Founder and editorial researcher

Published and checked: November 21, 2026

This article is based on new dermatology congress research, peer-reviewed reviews and American Academy of Dermatology guidance.

Stylish & Healthy6 min readResearch-first guide
Quick answer

The glass skin idea, gentle cleansing, hydration and barrier care, is sound. The viral version often isn't. A study presented at the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology (EADV) Congress in Vienna on 30 September 2026 found that almost 95% of popular glass skin videos on TikTok contained at least one misleading claim. A second study of 94 patients found that most of those harmed by social media routines were using several active ingredients at once. If your skin got worse after copying a routine, the usual fix is fewer products, not more.

What did the new research find?

Study 1: what TikTok is teaching

In May 2026, researchers used a brand-new TikTok account with no viewing history, so the algorithm couldn't tailor results, and looked at the top videos for five Korean skincare and glass skin searches. After exclusions, they assessed 38 videos for accuracy, safety information and reliability.1

  • 94.7% contained at least one potentially misleading claim.
  • 84% ignored differences between skin types.
  • 45% promoted heavy combinations of active ingredients without safety guidance.
  • 32% promised instant results.
  • Only one mentioned the risk of irritation, only two mentioned sun protection, and none cited a source or suggested seeing a dermatologist.

None of the videos were made by dermatologists. About 82% came from non-physician influencers and 11% from beauty brands.1

Study 2: who ended up in the clinic

A separate observational study looked at 94 patients who developed a new or worse skin problem after using products promoted on social media.1

What patients were usingShare of cases
AHA or BHA exfoliants34%
Over-the-counter retinoids27%
Vitamin C serums14%
Niacinamide products11%

Almost half (48%) had copied a complete routine from social media, and 63% were using more than three active ingredients at the same time. The reactions were mostly irritant contact dermatitis (42%) and new or much worse acne (28%), followed by rosacea flares, dark marks and some superficial chemical burns.1

How strong is this?

Both studies were conference presentations, so they haven't yet been through full peer review. The video sample was small, and the patient study only included people who already had problems, so it can't tell you how common reactions are. The researchers also stressed that Korean skincare itself isn't the issue.12 Still, the pattern matches what dermatologists have been reporting, including a rise in young patients reacting to products that aren't suitable for them.3

Why stacked routines backfire

The outer layer of your skin is a thin wall of flattened cells held together by fats. It keeps water in and irritants out. Exfoliating acids, retinoids, strong vitamin C and even niacinamide at high strengths can all loosen or inflame that layer a little. One at a time, that's usually manageable. Several at once, every day, and the effects add up. The researchers put it simply: many actives have overlapping irritant effects.1

There's a second problem. When skin gets irritated it often feels tight and looks dull or rough, so people add more hydrating layers, a new serum or another exfoliant to fix the dullness. That makes the original problem worse.

And the look itself can mislead. The "glass" shine in many videos comes from lighting, filters and dewy, occlusive layers sitting on the surface. It isn't proof that the skin underneath is healthy.

Signs your routine is doing too much

  • Products that never used to sting now burn, even plain moisturizer. (See why moisturizer burns.)
  • Skin looks shiny but feels tight, or looks "glassy" yet flakes around the nose and mouth.
  • New redness, rough patches or a sandpaper texture.
  • Small bumps or a breakout that started after you added several new products. Our guide to damaged barrier vs acne helps tell them apart.
  • Makeup and sunscreen suddenly start to pill over all the layers.
See a doctor if

you have blistering, swelling, oozing, pain, a rash that spreads, or a reaction near the eyes. Those need medical care, not a new routine.

A simpler routine that still glows

The parts of glass skin that are worth keeping are the boring ones: gentle cleansing, enough hydration and daily sunscreen. Moisturizers work by combining ingredients that attract water, smooth the surface and slow evaporation, so one good product often does what three thin layers were trying to do.4

Two-week reset
  1. Morning: rinse or use a gentle cleanser, moisturizer, then sunscreen.
  2. Evening: gentle cleanser, then moisturizer. Use a richer one if you're dry.
  3. Pause every active for about two weeks: acids, retinoids, vitamin C and exfoliating toners.
  4. Bring back one active at a time, a few nights a week, waiting two to three weeks before adding another.
  5. Patch test anything new before using it on your whole face.5

If you want the glow, add it at the end: a hydrating serum under your moisturizer, or a dewy sunscreen. Those give shine without stressing your skin. If you're oily but dehydrated, a light gel moisturizer usually does the job better than layering several toners.

Frequently asked questions

Is Korean skincare bad for your skin?

No. The researchers behind the new studies said Korean skincare isn't the problem. The risk comes from copying long, one-size-fits-all routines with many actives and no safety advice.

How many skincare products is too many?

There's no magic number, but the patient study found most people with reactions were using more than three active ingredients at once. Most skin does well with a cleanser, moisturizer, sunscreen and at most one or two actives.

Can you get glass skin without the 10 steps?

Smooth, well-hydrated skin mostly comes from not irritating it, moisturizing well and wearing sunscreen. The extra shine in videos often comes from lighting and dewy products sitting on top.

How long does it take skin to recover from too many actives?

Mild irritation often settles in one to four weeks once you simplify. If it doesn't, or if it's getting worse, see a dermatologist.

Are glass skin routines safe for teenagers?

Most teens don't need anti-ageing actives like retinoids or strong acids. Dermatologists have raised concerns about young people reacting to products that aren't designed for their skin.3 A gentle cleanser, moisturizer and sunscreen is enough unless a doctor suggests acne treatment.
