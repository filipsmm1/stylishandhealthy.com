Sunscreen or Bug Spray First? The Correct Order

the clean, science-backed answer for SPF, DEET, picaridin, kids, makeup, sweating and reapplication

By Filip · Founder and editorial researcher, Stylish & Healthy

Published: July 25, 2026 | Last updated: July 25, 2026

This article is not medically reviewed. It is researched from public-health guidance, regulatory documents and peer-reviewed studies, and is intended for education only.

Stylishandhealthy13 min readResearch-heavy guide
Quick answer

Apply sunscreen first, let it dry down, then apply insect repellent second. The reason is simple: sunscreen must form an even UV-protective film on the skin, while repellent is meant to sit closer to the outside surface where mosquitoes and ticks encounter it. If you are outdoors for hours, reapply sunscreen as directed, even if that means applying it over earlier repellent. Reapply repellent only when the label says to or when biting resumes.

The evidence-based order: sunscreen first, repellent second

The CDC gives the practical answer directly: when using both products, apply sunscreen first and insect repellent second.1 CDC travel guidance says the same thing for travelers and adds an important detail: use separate products, apply the sunscreen first, then the repellent.2

  1. Apply sunscreen generously to dry skin. Use enough product to cover all exposed areas evenly. Sunscreen under-application is one of the biggest real-world reasons people get less protection than the SPF on the label.
  2. Wait until the sunscreen film settles. A practical range is about 10 to 20 minutes, or until the sunscreen has dried down. The exact time depends on the formula.
  3. Apply insect repellent over exposed skin and clothing. Use just enough to cover exposed skin. Do not drench the skin. Do not spray directly into the face.
  4. Reapply sunscreen on schedule. Outdoors, that generally means at least every two hours, and sooner after swimming, sweating or towel drying depending on the sunscreen label.8
  5. Reapply repellent only when needed. Follow the repellent label. Repellent protection can last several hours, depending on the active ingredient, concentration, sweating, water exposure and insect pressure.
The non-negotiable point

Do not treat sunscreen and bug spray like two interchangeable skincare layers. One is a regulated UV-protection drug product in the U.S.; the other is a pesticide product regulated for repelling insects. They have different jobs, different application amounts and different reapplication schedules.

Why does sunscreen go first?

Sunscreen protection depends on an even film. Whether the filters are mineral, organic/chemical or a mixed formula, the product has to spread evenly across the skin surface and stay there. Rubbing another product underneath it can make the film uneven. Rubbing sunscreen over a fresh repellent layer can also smear, dilute or partially remove the repellent.

Repellents work differently. DEET, picaridin, IR3535 and PMD do not create SPF. Their job is to interfere with biting arthropods finding or landing on you. That is why public-health guidance puts repellent as the outer layer. CDC Yellow Book guidance also says not to spray repellent directly on the face; spray it onto the hands first, then apply it to the face.2

Film integritySunscreen needs even coverage

SPF testing assumes a controlled, uniform application. Real life already reduces that. Adding repellent underneath creates another variable.

Repellent placementRepellent belongs outside

Repellent works at the skin-air interface, where insects approach exposed skin. Applying it last is the practical logic.

Different schedulesThey do not expire together

Sunscreen may need reapplication every two hours or after water exposure. Repellent may not. That is why combination products are messy.

The key science issue: DEET can reduce measured SPF

The biggest evidence-based concern is not that sunscreen and repellent become “toxic” on the skin. The more practical concern is that repellent, especially DEET-containing repellent, can reduce sunscreen performance. CDC Yellow Book guidance states that limited data show DEET-containing insect repellents applied over sunscreen can decrease the sunscreen SPF by about one-third.2

The clinical-trial literature behind this concern is small, but it matters. A PubMed-indexed clinical trial notes that an earlier study found a 33.3% decrease in SPF when insect repellent and sunscreen were applied sequentially to the skin.12 That does not mean every sunscreen-repellent combination loses exactly one-third of its protection. It means the risk is plausible enough that public-health agencies advise extra caution.

DEET, picaridin, IR3535 and PMD: what changes?

The EPA lists several active ingredients used in EPA-registered skin-applied repellents, including DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus, PMD and 2-undecanone.7 CDC also lists EPA-registered active ingredients and states that, when used as directed, EPA-registered repellents are proven safe and effective, including for pregnant and breastfeeding people.1

What about absorption and “mixing chemicals”?

Some laboratory and animal studies have examined DEET and oxybenzone together. In vitro work found that concurrent application of commercially available repellent and sunscreen preparations could significantly increase percutaneous permeation of DEET and oxybenzone across piglet skin.13 Other studies have compared DEET and picaridin with oxybenzone preparations.15

This is useful mechanistic evidence, but it has limits. In vitro diffusion studies, pig skin and rat studies do not automatically predict the exact risk for a person applying modern sunscreen and repellent during one afternoon outdoors. The practical takeaway is still conservative: avoid unnecessary over-application, avoid combination products, wash treated skin after exposure, and do not apply repellents over irritated, cut or wounded skin.

Do not overinterpret the lab studies

“Absorption can occur” is not the same as “normal use is dangerous.” EPA’s DEET review concludes no risks of concern for registered dermal repellent use when used as directed.5 The safety strategy is not panic; it is correct label use.

Why sunscreen + insect repellent combination products are usually a bad idea

Combination products sound convenient, but the dosing logic is bad. Sunscreen labels call for liberal or generous application and frequent reapplication. Repellent labels usually say to use just enough to cover exposed skin and to avoid over-application. FDA’s proposed sunscreen order discusses this mismatch and notes that sunscreen-repellent combination products raise unresolved safety and effectiveness questions.10

Verdict

Use separate products. A two-in-one product can push you toward either too little sunscreen or too much repellent. That is especially relevant for children because sunscreen often needs to be reapplied far more often than repellent.

How to reapply during a real outdoor day

This is the part most articles answer badly. They say “sunscreen first, repellent second,” but then ignore the fact that sunscreen often needs reapplication before repellent does. CDC Yellow Book sun-exposure guidance says sunscreen may be reapplied over insect repellent every two hours during long-term sun exposure.4

Scenario 1: You are outside for less than two hours

  1. Apply sunscreen before leaving.
  2. Let it dry down.
  3. Apply repellent to exposed skin and clothing.
  4. Wash treated skin when you come inside.

Scenario 2: You are outside all afternoon

  1. Apply sunscreen first and repellent second before exposure.
  2. At the two-hour mark, reapply sunscreen to exposed sun-exposed areas, even if repellent was applied earlier.
  3. Do not automatically reapply repellent every time you reapply sunscreen. Check the repellent label and whether biting has resumed.
  4. If you swim, sweat heavily or towel dry, follow the sunscreen water-resistance timing, usually 40 or 80 minutes depending on label claims.
  5. If repellent washes off or biting resumes, reapply repellent after the sunscreen has settled again.

Scenario 3: You are swimming, sweating or working outdoors

Water-resistant sunscreen labels in the U.S. are tied to 40 or 80 minutes of swimming or sweating, and FDA-required directions include reapplication after that period, immediately after towel drying, and at least every two hours.9 There is no “waterproof” or “sweatproof” sunscreen claim in the FDA system because those claims overstate protection.

  • Use water-resistant broad-spectrum sunscreen for swimming or sweating.
  • Use sun-protective clothing to reduce how much skin needs both products.
  • After swimming, towel drying or heavy sweating, rebuild the sunscreen film first.
  • Reapply repellent afterward only where insects are biting exposed skin.
  • Do not spray repellent under clothing unless the label specifically allows clothing application.

Mineral vs chemical sunscreen: does the order change?

No. The order stays the same: sunscreen first, repellent second. Mineral sunscreens use zinc oxide and/or titanium dioxide; organic/chemical sunscreens use UV filters such as avobenzone, octocrylene, homosalate or other region-dependent filters. But both depend on an even protective layer across skin.

Kids, pregnancy, acne, eczema and sensitive skin

Children

The American Academy of Pediatrics says that if separate sunscreen and insect repellent are used, sunscreen should go first and insect repellent second.16 AAP child-safety guidance also advises against applying repellent to children’s hands, eyes, mouth, cuts or irritated skin, and against combination sunscreen/repellent products.17

  • Adults should apply repellent for young children.
  • Do not spray repellent directly on a child’s face. Spray into adult hands first, then apply carefully.
  • Do not apply repellent to children’s hands because hands go into eyes and mouth.
  • Do not use oil of lemon eucalyptus or PMD on children under 3 years old, per CDC Yellow Book guidance.2
  • Use clothing, hats, stroller nets and shade to reduce the amount of product needed.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

CDC states that EPA-registered insect repellents are proven safe and effective when used as directed, including for pregnant and breastfeeding women.1 That matters because mosquito-borne infections can be serious. The safer move is not skipping protection; it is using registered products according to the label.

Acne-prone skin

Repellent is not an acne treatment and does not belong in a delicate skincare routine unless you actually need insect protection. If your face breaks out easily, use repellent mostly on exposed body skin and clothing when possible. For the face, apply the smallest effective amount, avoid oily over-application, and cleanse gently after exposure.

Eczema, rosacea or sensitive skin

EPA says never to use repellents over cuts, wounds or irritated skin.6 That is especially relevant for eczema flares, shaving irritation, sunburn, barrier damage or active dermatitis. Fragrance-heavy “natural” sprays are not automatically safer; essential oils can be irritating or allergenic.

Sensitive-skin strategy

Use clothing first, then sunscreen on exposed skin, then a low-fragrance EPA-registered repellent only where needed. Patch test before a long trip, especially if you react easily.

What if you wear makeup?

Makeup makes this more annoying. The cleanest evidence-based sequence is still sunscreen first and repellent second, but your makeup layer complicates film formation and reapplication.

  1. Morning base: skincare, sunscreen, wait, makeup.
  2. Before outdoor insect exposure: apply repellent to exposed body skin and clothing. For the face, spray repellent into hands and pat carefully around the perimeter, avoiding eyes and mouth.
  3. Reapplying sunscreen: use the method that gives actual coverage: lotion if possible, or a generous SPF stick/cream/powder as a backup only when lotion is impractical.
  4. Reapplying repellent: only when needed and never by spraying a cloud into your face.

Blunt answer: if you are in strong sun plus heavy mosquitoes, perfect makeup is not the priority. Skin cancer prevention, sunburn prevention and bite prevention win.

Common myths, corrected

MythReality
“Bug spray goes first because it needs skin contact.”No. Public-health guidance says sunscreen first, repellent second. Repellent works as the outer protective layer.
“DEET is always unsafe.”No. EPA’s risk assessment supports registered DEET use when used as directed. Misuse, ingestion, eye exposure and over-application are the real problems.
“Natural repellent is always gentler.”No. Essential oils can irritate skin and may not be EPA-registered for disease-risk protection.
“Combination SPF bug spray saves time.”Convenient, yes. Good dosing logic, usually no. Sunscreen and repellent have different application amounts and reapplication schedules.
“Water-resistant means waterproof.”No. FDA does not allow “waterproof” or “sweatproof” sunscreen claims. Water resistance is time-limited.
“If I use SPF 100, I can ignore reapplication.”No. Higher SPF does not fix rubbing, sweat, water exposure or uneven application.

The simplest routine that actually makes sense

Sunscreen first. Wait. Repellent second. Sunscreen reapplication stays on its own clock.

For a normal summer walk

  • Broad-spectrum SPF 30+ on exposed skin.
  • Wait until dry.
  • EPA-registered repellent on exposed skin and clothing where needed.
  • Wash off repellent when you come inside.

For hiking, camping, travel or outdoor work

  • Use water-resistant SPF 30+ or SPF 50.

FAQ

Should sunscreen or bug spray go first?

Sunscreen first, insect repellent second. This is the CDC/AAP-aligned order.

How long should I wait?

Wait until the sunscreen dries down. Practically, that is often around 10 to 20 minutes.

Can I reapply sunscreen over repellent?

Yes. During long sun exposure, reapply sunscreen as directed, even if repellent was applied earlier.

Should I reapply bug spray every two hours?

Not automatically. Repellent should be reapplied according to the product label or when biting resumes.

Can I use DEET with sunscreen?

Yes, when used correctly. Apply sunscreen first and repellent second, and be careful with sunscreen reapplication because DEET may lower measured SPF.

Is picaridin better with sunscreen?

It may feel more cosmetic than DEET, but the order does not change. There is less direct SPF-interaction evidence than for DEET.

Can kids use sunscreen and repellent together?

Yes, with age-appropriate products and adult application. Avoid combination products, hands, eyes, mouth, cuts and irritated skin.

Should I wash bug spray off?

Yes. After outdoor exposure, wash treated skin with soap and water, especially before bed.
